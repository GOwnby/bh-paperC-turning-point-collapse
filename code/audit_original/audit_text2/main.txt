             Collapse Toward a Regularized Core:
      General-Relativistic Instability of a Degenerate Star
              Near Its Effective Maximum Mass
                                        Gracen Ownby


                                            Abstract
     Paper B in this series shows that no known classical field theory sources the mass-dependent
regularized core the rest of the series studies, within general relativity coupled to standard mat-
ter. This leaves open a dynamical question Paper B does not address: setting the sourcing
problem aside, does realistic gravitational collapse even approach the regime where such a core
would matter, or does ordinary physics intervene first? We study this for the most natural
candidate route, a degenerate stellar core collapsing near its effective maximum mass. A first
attempt, using the real Chandrasekhar degenerate-electron equation of state extended with a
simplified, single-nuclide neutronization treatment, found a genuine maximum mass and a New-
tonian radial-pulsation instability there; comparing against precision literature then showed
this equation of state deviates from the real physics by an order of magnitude in density, be-
cause general relativity, not neutronization, is the mechanism that actually limits a Carbon-12
white dwarf’s mass. Correcting this — a general-relativistic (TOV) equilibrium structure using
the pure degenerate-electron equation of state, validated against the historical Chandrasekhar–
Tooper critical mass to 0.07% — changes the configuration but not the conclusion. Applying our
already-validated Newtonian pulsation solver to the corrected structure finds the local adiabatic
index everywhere above the classical Pekeris threshold, apparently stable; but the corrected con-
figuration sits past its own mass maximum, where the rigorously proven turning-point theorem
places the onset of dynamical instability at the mass maximum and so implies instability beyond
it, through a mechanism (the Chandrasekhar–Tooper effect) that is intrinsically relativistic and
invisible to Newtonian theory. Settling this directly required implementing Chandrasekhar’s
own general-relativistic pulsation equation from his 1964 variational principle, a substantially
harder calculation that we document honestly, including several genuine implementation er-
rors found and corrected along the way and validated, before trusting any new result, against
both the Newtonian limit (better than 99.9% agreement at low compactness) and the historical
critical mass. The calculation confirms instability directly, and an exact external test — the
turning-point theorem, which requires the fundamental eigenvalue to vanish exactly where the
mass peaks — is now passed to 0.01% in the critical density for five equations of state, after
that same test exposed and led us to correct two errors in our first implementation (a miss-
ing term in Chandrasekhar’s potential, and an energy density inconsistent with the first law of
thermodynamics). Two trial functions agree to within 1% (σ 2 ≈ −28 s−2 ), each individually
sufficient for instability by Chandrasekhar’s own variational principle; Rayleigh–Ritz tightens
this to a converged σ 2 = −28.55 s−2 , e-folding time 0.187 s (our earlier value, −41.05 s−2 ,
is superseded). This is a more carefully grounded, independently confirmed dynamical fact
about the matter route to this regularization scale than our first attempt, resting on the ac-
tual relativistic mechanism rather than an artifact of an over-simplified equation of state. We
also checked the Newtonian canonical-energy criterion of Friedman against the classical ho-
mologous pulsation constant (both vanish at exactly Γ1 = 4/3 and agree in sign); identifying
the relativistic unstable mode as a negative-canonical-energy perturbation rests on Friedman’s
general result together with Sorkin’s theorem, not on this check alone. On the preliminary con-
figuration, an independent Eulerian hydrodynamics code evolved cleanly through a numerical

     resolution-converged even under the mathematically correct pure-growing-mode initial condi-

     be TOV-consistent, resolves the resolution-dependence of the qualitative outcome and repro-

     quantitative growth rate, a discrepancy traced to a numerical-stiffness obstacle in an otherwise

     numerical-relativity literature instead: full 3+1 evolutions of stars just past their own turning

     migrating to the stable branch under an outward one, confirmed as a critical phenomenon in
     subsequent work. This retroactively explains our own code’s opposite-sign-seed divergence as
     physical rather than numerical, and, for the astrophysically realized (compressive) direction this
     series’ scenario actually involves, establishes genuine, non-saturating collapse — not a bounded
     oscillation and not migration to safety — as the settled qualitative outcome. We then obtain
     an actual quantitative collapse timescale, without needing the strong-form code’s delicate can-
     cellation (gravity and pressure terms individually enormous, differing by parts in a thousand)
     to converge: a single-degree-of-freedom nonlinear model, built as the nonlinear continuation
     of Chandrasekhar’s own homologous trial function and validated term by term against exact
     analytic results, with its approximate frozen-background treatment of self-gravity calibrated
     transparently, once, against the independently validated linear eigenvalue and cross-checked
     against an exact-energy construction. Integrated forward, it reproduces the validated e-folding
     time (0.19 s) during an initial slow exponential phase, then runs away on the star’s own dynam-
     ical timescale (∼ 0.03 s) once the perturbation becomes of order unity, giving a total collapse
     time of order a few seconds for generic small seed perturbations. These two numbers rest on
     different footing, and we are explicit about which is which: the e-folding time is an essentially
     direct consequence of the independently validated linear eigenvalue, while the plunge duration
     and total collapse time are extrapolations of a calibration fixed only at the linear level, offered
     as an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a precision result, explicitly reported as logarith-
     mically sensitive to the (astrophysically undetermined) seed size for a star placed beyond the
     turning point, and explicitly not pushed through the compactness threshold at which even the
     calibrated frozen-metric treatment can no longer be trusted. For the physically relevant case of
     a star reaching the maximum mass by slow accretion, a ramped saddle-node analysis built on
     the same exact-GR coefficients (Painlevé-I scaling, checked against the homologous model to
     0.03%) removes the seed ambiguity and gives a delay of minutes after Mmax is crossed, scaling
     as Ṁ −1/5 , with collapse at Mmax to better than 10−10 M⊙ .


1    Introduction
This series studies a Hayward-type regular black hole whose regularization scale is tied to the
mass, ε3 = 2GM ℓ2 /c2 , so that the core density is independent of M . Paper A studies the exterior:
evaporation toward the critical mass at which the metric’s horizons merge, and the fate of the
radiation’s entanglement entropy. Paper B asks what sources the core, and finds that nothing
classical does — not a fluid with the required equation of state, not nonlinear electrodynamics,
not a minimally or non-minimally coupled scalar field, alone or in combination, within general
relativity. That is a statement about which static configurations general relativity can support; it
says nothing about whether a real collapsing star would ever reach the regime where the question
matters in the first place.
    This paper asks the dynamical question directly, for the most physically motivated candidate:
a degenerate stellar core, collapsing under its own gravity, pushed past the point where degeneracy
pressure alone can hold it up. The qualitative story is old and well established — white dwarfs


                                                     2
above an effective maximum mass are unstable, and the instability is tied to the adiabatic index
crossing the classical Pekeris threshold Γ1 = 4/3 [Pekeris, 1938, Chandrasekhar, 1935]. What is not
established, and what this paper computes directly, is whether this generic story holds quantita-
tively for the specific matter model and specific configuration that would need to produce something
resembling the regularized core of Papers A and B: a real degenerate equation of state, softened
by neutronization as it approaches the relevant densities, evolved as a genuinely inhomogeneous
general-relativistic collapse rather than a toy homogeneous one.
    We build this up in stages, including a real correction along the way. Section 2 derives the cor-
rect equations for inhomogeneous collapse, including a gauge subtlety that explains why a simpler
homogeneous treatment cannot just be extended. Section 3 builds the real equation of state, finds
a genuine maximum-mass turnover from a first, simplified neutronization treatment, then shows
that treatment deviates from precision literature by an order of magnitude and corrects it using
the actual dominant (general-relativistic) mechanism, validated against a historical result before
being trusted. Section 4 summarizes, for the preliminary configuration, what two independent
nonlinear numerical strategies show and do not show about the resulting dynamics; both encounter
real, instructive limitations, documented in full in the companion technical report [Ownby, 2026].
Section 5 is the paper’s linear-stability core: it begins with a Newtonian linear-stability calculation
that turns out, applied honestly to the corrected structure, not to see an instability that must
nonetheless be present, which motivates implementing Chandrasekhar’s actual general-relativistic
pulsation equation — a substantially harder calculation, whose own debugging history we report in
full — to settle the question unambiguously; Section 5.9 then tests it against an exact result (the
turning-point theorem) and reports the two errors that test exposed in our first implementation.
Section 6 reports a direct, carefully controlled nonlinear test, first on the preliminary configura-
tion and then redone on the corrected one with a properly TOV-consistent nonlinear code, and
an honest account of what each does and does not resolve. Section 7 then settles the qualitative
nonlinear question — not by further patching that code, but by connecting our validated linear
eigenvalue to Friedman’s canonical-energy criterion and to established numerical-relativity liter-
ature on exactly this class of configuration. Section 8 goes on to obtain an actual quantitative
collapse timescale, using a single-degree-of-freedom nonlinear model built as the nonlinear contin-
uation of Chandrasekhar’s own homologous trial function, validated term by term and calibrated,
transparently, against the independently known linear eigenvalue. The same section also reports
a two-mode extension (Section 8.4) a composition and lattice-correction survey (Section 8.5), and
the dynamic fold passage of a star reaching Mmax by slow accretion (Section 8.6), which replaces
the free seed amplitude by one set by the accretion rate. Section 9 discusses what all of this means
for the series, including what the correction changes and what it does not.


2     Setup: inhomogeneous collapse in general relativity
2.1   Why the homogeneous treatment cannot simply be extended
Paper B’s collapse calculation uses a homogeneous interior matched to the exterior mass function at
the stellar surface. Genuinely inhomogeneous collapse requires the areal radius R, and the pressure
and density, to depend on the comoving radial label r as well as time τ . A first attempt at the
natural generalization,
                              ds2 = −dτ 2 + X(τ, r)2 dr2 + R(τ, r)2 dΩ2 ,                       (1)
a fully synchronous, unit-lapse, comoving gauge, fails for a specific and informative reason. Direct
computation of ∇µ T µ r = 0 for a perfect fluid comoving with this metric gives, exactly, ∂p/∂r = 0.

The metric is reporting that a unit lapse is only consistent with a pressure-free source (dust)
or one with spatially uniform pressure — exactly the two cases already covered without genuine
inhomogeneity. Physically, a fluid with a genuine pressure gradient has non-geodesic worldlines,
since the pressure gradient accelerates fluid elements relative to free fall, and a unit-lapse metric
cannot represent that.

                                                       4
3.2   Neutronization, and a genuine maximum mass
Above ρ ∼ 1012 –1013 kg/m3 , electron capture onto nuclei reduces the electron fraction Ye and soft-
ens the pressure below the pure-electron-gas value. We implement a bounded, physically grounded
simplification of the full treatment [Baym et al., 1971]: fixing the mass number at A = 12 and find-
ing, at each baryon density, the charge Z that minimizes total energy per baryon (nuclear mass via
the semi-empirical mass formula, plus the electron energy already derived above). This reproduces
the correct zero-density beta-stability charge (Zeq = 5.77 for A = 12, close to carbon’s Z = 6)
and the correct threshold density scale for the onset of softening, from real nuclear binding-energy
differences rather than an assumed cutoff.
    The consequence was not anticipated when this calculation was set up: rebuilding the Newtonian
hydrostatic sequence with this softened equation of state produces a genuine maximum mass,
Mmax ≈ 1.214 M⊙ at ρc ≈ 2.3 × 1012 kg/m3 , with mass decreasing for higher central density —
qualitatively the correct physics of why sufficiently dense white dwarfs are dynamically unstable
before the naive Chandrasekhar limit.

    This specific quantitative result turned out not to survive contact with precision literature, and it
is worth being direct about why. Comparing against Rotondo, Rueda, Ruffini & Xue [Rotondo et al.,
2011], a precision treatment combining the full TOV equation, self-consistent β-equilibrium, and
proper Coulomb lattice corrections, gives for Carbon-12 specifically ρcrit ≈ 2.12 × 1013 kg/m3 and
Mcrit ≈ 1.386 M⊙ — our values above are off by a factor of ∼ 9 in density and ∼ 12% in mass. The
reason is identifiable: that paper shows that for Carbon-12 specifically, the general-relativistic insta-
bility (Chandrasekhar & Tooper [Chandrasekhar and Tooper, 1964]) sets in before neutronization
does, so GR is the dominant mechanism for this composition, not the one this section included.
Our simplified, single-nuclide, no-Coulomb-correction SEMF treatment also independently turns

way: this section’s specific numbers are not quantitatively trustworthy, even though its qualitative

the dominant (general-relativistic) mechanism instead.

3.3   The dominant mechanism is general-relativistic, not neutronization, and
      the corrected structure
Since Rotondo et al. identify general relativity, not neutronization, as the mechanism that actually
limits a Carbon-12 white dwarf’s mass, we redo the equilibrium structure using the pure (non-
neutronized) Chandrasekhar equation of state — already validated above against the Newtonian
Chandrasekhar mass — with the TOV equation instead of Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium,
dropping neutronization. This is self-consistent at the density where the mechanism sets in for 4 He
and 12 C, but not at the fiducial test density below for 12 C, which lies above its inverse-β threshold
(Section 8.5); neutronization there would soften the equation of state further, which we do not
model.
    We validated this directly against history before trusting it on anything new: Chandrasekhar &
Tooper [Chandrasekhar and Tooper, 1964] computed the critical mass for this exact combination
(pure degenerate-electron EOS, full GR) as Mcrit = 1.4176 M⊙ . Our TOV sequence, with the
thermodynamically consistent energy density described below, gives Mmax = 1.4166 M⊙ at ρc =
2.31 × 1013 kg/m3 — agreement to 0.07% (with M⊙ = 1.98841 × 1030 kg; the same value 1.4166 M⊙
is quoted for this problem in the recent literature, arXiv:1401.0819), using independently written
code, not tuned to reproduce this number. (An earlier version of this calculation, which took the
rest-mass density as the energy density, gave 1.4252 M⊙ , 0.5% off, with the maximum misplaced to
3.6 × 1013 kg/m3 ; see Section 5.9.)

5.4   Why this Newtonian answer is not the final one
Section 3.3 showed the preliminary configuration’s own equation of state was not quantitatively
trustworthy. Applying the identical, already-validated Newtonian solver above to the corrected
configuration (ρc = 5 × 1013 kg/m3 , pure Chandrasekhar EOS, full TOV background) gives a real
surprise: Γ1 stays above 4/3 everywhere in the star, bottoming out at 1.33409 at the center — just
barely above the Pekeris threshold, never crossing it. The Newtonian solver, applied honestly to
this background, reports the configuration stable.
     This cannot be the end of the story. The corrected configuration sits past its own mass maximum
(ρc = 2.31 × 1013 kg/m3 , Section 3.3), and the turning-point theorem of relativistic stellar stability
— proved rigorously by Sorkin [Sorkin, 1981], not a heuristic — places the onset of instability at the
first mass maximum (the classical result of Harrison, Thorne, Wakano & Wheeler [Harrison et al.,
1965] extends this along the sequence), so a configuration past it is dynamically unstable, with
the fundamental mode’s frequency passing through zero exactly at the turning point. Something
must be unstable here; Newtonian perturbation theory, by construction, cannot see it, because the
instability is a property of the dynamics in full general relativity, not merely of the local value of Γ1
against the Newtonian threshold. The Chandrasekhar–Tooper mechanism validated numerically
in Section 3.3 is exactly this: a star can be GR-unstable with Γ1 arbitrarily close to, but still
above, 4/3, provided the excess is small enough relative to the star’s compactness. Settling this
rigorously requires the actual general-relativistic pulsation equation, not the Newtonian one applied
to a relativistic background.

5.6   Getting the implementation right: a genuine, instructive debugging history
Reaching Eq. (14) took real, repeated effort, and we report the failures as well as the fix, since both
are informative. A direct shooting integration of the equivalent first-order system was numerically
unstable in our first attempt (we judged the mode by the surface value of the displacement; an
independent re-implementation later showed that shooting works well, to 10−9 , when the regular-
surface condition is imposed on the Lagrangian pressure variation P ζ ′ near the surface and a
high-order integrator is used). A naive strong-form finite-difference discretization, in raw SI units,
produced only non-finite eigenvalues, traced to Pi, Q, and S coefficients spanning thirty orders
of magnitude — a conditioning failure, not a physics error. Row-and-column equilibration fixed
the conditioning but produced spurious, grid-scale-dominated spectra (lowest-|σ 2 | eigenvectors with
hundreds of nodes), the same pathology a non-weak-form discretization produced for the Newtonian
problem in Section 5.2. Deriving and symbolically verifying a properly symmetric weak form fixed
the spurious-mode problem but, when checked directly against the already-validated Newtonian
solver in the low-compactness limit, disagreed by three to four orders of magnitude, with the
disagreement not converging to unity as compactness was taken to zero — ruling out a subtle
missing higher-order correction and pointing to a genuine coefficient error.
    The error, once found, was a single mis-specified conversion factor: in passing from the equa-
tion’s natural variable ζ = e−a r2 ξ back to ξ when assembling one of the two bilinear terms, we
applied the ξ → ζ conversion twice rather than once, having already tested with the ζ-type com-
bination. Comparing our transcription term-by-term against an independently published, explicit
restatement of Eq. (14) [Haemmerlé, 2025] located the first mismatch. Implementing that refer-
ence’s explicit form directly, rather than continuing to patch our own derivation, resolved that
error — though, as Section 5.9 reports, that comparison itself missed one term: at compactness
∼ 5 × 10−5 , the corrected calculation agrees with the Newtonian limit to 99.98%, with the expected,
physically sensible departure as compactness increases (Figure 2, left panel).

5.8    Tightening the estimate: Rayleigh–Ritz
A single trial function gives only an upper bound on the true fundamental eigenvalue. The textbook
way to improve it — the method the variational principle itself is built for [Chandrasekhar, 1964b] —
is Rayleigh–Ritz: expand ξ in a small basisR of trial functions satisfying theRcorrect center regularity,
assemble the full matrix elements Aij = [Π ζi′ ζj′ + Qξi ξj ] dr and Bij = Sξi ξj dr (not just their
diagonal, single-trial values), and solve the resulting small generalized eigenvalue problem. We
verified A, B symmetric to machine precision before trusting any eigenvalue from them, exactly as
for the original Newtonian and relativistic solvers.

5.9   An exact external test, and the two errors it exposed

6.5    A precision-exact reformulation: real progress, and a new obstacle it ex-
       posed
The quantitative mismatch above has a specific, identifiable cause worth taking seriously rather
than leaving as an open puzzle. This instability’s slowness is itself a delicate fact: Γ1 exceeds 4/3
by only 0.0008 at the center, which is why the net restoring term is only about 0.4% of either of
the two large terms it is the difference of (3Γ1 − 4 ≈ 0.008). A numerical scheme that represents
gravity and pressure as two separately discretized quantities — an analytic source term evaluated
pointwise, and a Riemann-solver flux divergence — must rely on those two large, independently-
computed numbers cancelling to the same precision the physics does. Nothing in Section 6.4’s
scheme enforces that; it is a coincidence of floating-point arithmetic whether it happens, and the
evidence above (the drift test, the dynamical-timescale match) suggests it mostly does not.
    The natural fix is to never represent the large cancellation in floating point at all: evolve the
perturbation variables δρ ≡ ρ−ρ0 (r) and the momentum directly, with the background equilibrium
subtracted analytically, symbolically, before any number is computed. Using the background’s own
exact force balance, dP0 /dr = ρ0 g(r, ρ0 , P0 ), the momentum equation becomes

Two more genuine bugs, caught by the same discipline. Checking the discretized mo-

test would have missed. The momentum equation passed outright: zero to the last bit, confirming
the analytic-subtraction approach works exactly as intended. The continuity equation did not.
Tracing it down: a standard Riemann solver’s numerical diffusion, designed to stabilize genuine
density discontinuities, fires spuriously on any smoothly-varying background, since adjacent re-
constructed cell states necessarily differ even when the true solution is perfectly smooth and the
physical flux is identically zero. The fix is to evaluate the background density analytically at each
cell interface (available for free, since ρ0 (r) is a known function, not reconstructed from a solve)
rather than through the Riemann solver’s reconstruction, applying the solver’s genuine machinery
only to the perturbation piece where discontinuities could plausibly develop.

Result: the equilibrium problem is solved outright. With both fixes in place, the un-
perturbed background shows exactly zero drift in both equations, sustained over many dynamical
times, at every resolution tested — not a converging approximation but an exact statement, verified

solved, not a failure of the same kind: it is a standard stiff-ODE problem, and the standard remedy
is an implicit or semi-implicit treatment of the gravitational source term, not further patches to
an explicit scheme. We did not implement one. We report this honestly as the natural next step,
rather than letting the real progress above (an exactly-preserved equilibrium) imply a growth-rate
result we do not have.


7    The nonlinear outcome: what established literature, rather than
     further ad hoc numerics, settles
Sections 6–6.5 tried repeatedly to answer “what does the star actually do?” by building an in-
creasingly careful from-scratch nonlinear code, and repeatedly ran into new numerical obstacles
(stiffness, equilibrium drift, broadband contamination of the seed) before reaching a trustworthy
nonlinear answer. Rather than continuing that pattern, we asked whether the physical question —
the nonlinear fate of a star with a confirmed negative-canonical-energy mode (Section 5.10), sitting
just past a turning point (Sorkin, Section 5.4) — is already settled in the literature on exactly this


                                                  22
class of configuration. It is, and the answer is more precise, and more informative, than a simple
“it collapses.”

7.1   The established result: a genuine bifurcation, not a universal fate
Full numerical-relativity evolutions of spherical stars placed just past their own equilibrium mass
maximum show that the nonlinear fate is governed by the sign of the perturbation along the
unstable mode, not fixed by instability alone. Font et al. [Font et al., 2002], evolving TOV stars
on the unstable branch in full 3+1 numerical relativity, find explicitly that such a star “can either
collapse to a black hole or expand and migrate to the stable branch,” depending on which direction
it is pushed; under their code’s own truncation-error-level perturbation it migrates outward rather
than collapsing, and a deliberate inward perturbation is needed to see collapse. Radice, Rezzolla
& Kellermann [Radice et al., 2010], studying this bifurcation in detail as a critical phenomenon,
confirm the same structure quantitatively: models perturbed outward behave as if they were on the
stable branch (“a suitably perturbed supercritical model can behave as a subcritical one”), while
models perturbed inward collapse to a black hole, with larger inward perturbations collapsing faster.
Neither finding is specific to one equation of state; both papers frame it as a generic consequence
of sitting on the unstable branch of a one-parameter equilibrium sequence with an accessible stable
branch nearby, which is exactly the structure Sorkin’s theorem guarantees generically (a mass
maximum separating a stable branch at lower central density from an unstable one at higher
central density, for any such sequence).
     This is not a new, unrelated finding we are importing by analogy: it is the established,
numerically-confirmed nonlinear continuation of precisely the configuration Section 5.10 diagnosed.
It also retroactively explains, rather than leaves unexplained, our own Section 6–6.4 finding that
identical-shape seeds of opposite sign drove our nonlinear code to opposite fates (rarefaction versus
runaway collapse): that sign-dependence is not a numerical artifact or a sign of an unconverged
scheme, as we were careful to hedge at the time, but the correct, literature-predicted qualitative
behavior of a star sitting on this part of an equilibrium sequence. Our own nonlinear investiga-
tion’s qualitative finding was right; what it lacked was the quantitative growth rate and a principled
way to know the qualitative split was physical rather than a bug, which the literature above now
supplies.

7.2   Which branch is astrophysically realized here
The bifurcation above is symmetric in the mathematics but not in the astrophysics this paper’s
scenario describes. The question motivating this whole series’ matter route (Section 2) is whether
a star can be driven, by ordinary physical processes — continued accretion, continued loss of
pressure support as degeneracy and eventual neutronization proceed — up through and past its
own maximum mass. That physical process is compressive: it pushes the star’s central density up,
in the same direction as the collapsing branch of Section 7.1, not the rarefaction branch. A star
that reaches the turning point by having matter added to it, or by its pressure support failing, does
not simultaneously receive a fortuitous outward kick of the kind needed to migrate back onto the
stable branch; the perturbation astrophysically realized in this scenario is the one that [Font et al.,
2002, Radice et al., 2010] show collapses.
    This licenses a specific, appropriately qualified conclusion: for the scenario this paper and this
series actually care about — continued, physically ordinary collapse or accretion driving a de-
generate star past the Chandrasekhar–Tooper threshold validated in Section 5.7 — the nonlinear
outcome is genuine, non-saturating gravitational collapse, not a bounded oscillation and not migra-


                                                  23
tion to a lower-density equilibrium. This conclusion rests on (i) our own validated linear result that
the configuration has a negative-canonical-energy fundamental mode (Sections 5.7–5.10), and (ii)
established numerical-relativity literature on the generic bifurcation structure of exactly this class
of configuration, not on our own from-scratch nonlinear code, whose repeated numerical obstacles
(Sections 6–6.5) we no longer need it to overcome in order to answer this question. We did not
ourselves run a full numerical-relativity evolution of this specific equation of state through to col-
lapse or migration; we rely on the established genericity of the bifurcation across the configurations
[Font et al., 2002, Radice et al., 2010] actually tested, which is a real but explicit limitation, stated
here rather than left implicit.


8     A quantitative nonlinear collapse timescale: a calibrated, order-
      of-magnitude estimate
Section 7 settled the qualitative question — collapse, not migration, for the astrophysically realized
perturbation direction — using established literature rather than our own nonlinear code, whose
repeated numerical obstacles (Sections 6–6.5) we traced to a specific, diagnosed cause: this instabil-
ity’s growth rate is a residual of two terms (pressure support and self-gravity) that are individually
enormous and nearly cancel, because Γ1 exceeds 4/3 by only a few parts in a thousand. A strong-
form, spatially discretized evolution code represents both terms as large numbers of comparable size
and subtracts them at every grid point at every timestep; we confirmed directly, by rebuilding that
approach from scratch in the present, independent verification, that this produces a Jacobian with
extremely large condition number at every resolution tested, with a genuine near-zero singular value
(not merely poor scaling) and a leading eigenvalue that drifts with resolution instead of converging
— the same pathology class the original investigation’s history (Section 6.5) already encountered
and did not resolve. We did not continue attempting to repair that approach. This section reports
what we built in its place: a single-degree-of-freedom nonlinear model, validated piece by piece
against exact analytic results and against the independently-confirmed linear eigenvalue, used to
extract an actual quantitative collapse timescale rather than only its sign.

8.1   The model: a nonlinear homologous extension of Chandrasekhar’s own trial

trial functions, and the Rayleigh–Ritz combination of five basis functions built from them (Sec-

EOB’s calibration targets are themselves independent nonlinear numerical-relativity results, so a
successful calibration carries some evidence that the model’s nonlinear behavior, not just its linear
limit, is being captured correctly. Our only calibration target is the linear eigenvalue itself; nothing
independent constrains the model in the nonlinear regime the plunge phase (Section 8.3) actually

elsewhere, and Section 8.2 already reflects this by treating the calibration as fixing a slope, not a
validated nonlinear behavior.

which requires a genuinely inhomogeneous, strong-field evolution.

8.6   Reaching the fold by quasi-static accretion: the dynamic delay

arbitrary seed δ0 . A star that approaches Mmax by slow accretion never sits there: it arrives along

8.7    Dissipation in the fold passage
The fold passage above is conservative. We now ask what dissipation can change. Four channels
are considered, and the answer is a bound plus an explicit requirement on microphysics we do not
compute.

What cannot change. Dissipation does not move fixed points: the equilibrium sequence, and
hence ρmax and Mmax , are those of the equilibrium equation of state, provided the matter relaxes
to equilibrium in a time short compared with β (otherwise the relevant sequence is the one at
frozen composition, with a different turning point). Gravitational radiation from a radial mode
vanishes in spherical symmetry (Birkhoff), and shear viscosity does not act on a homologous radial
motion. Thermal non-adiabaticity is also negligible here: for degenerate electrons the pressure is
nearly independent of temperature, so heat exchange between fluid elements does not change Γ1
at leading order. At ρmax the electron Fermi energy is 11.2 MeV, so (kB T /EF )2 = 6 × 10−7 at
T = 108 K, and the ion thermal pressure is 1.5 × 10−3 , 4.9 × 10−4 , 3.7 × 10−4 and 1.1 × 10−4 of the
electron pressure for 4 He, 12 C, 16 O and 56 Fe. That ion term is a finite-temperature correction to
the equation of state itself (we use T = 0), not a dissipation, and we do not evaluate its effect on
Mmax .

8.10    What this does, and does not, establish
This calculation is a genuine nonlinear result, not merely the linear eigenvalue restated: it evolves
the fully nonlinear internal energy (exact, via the barotropic identity, with no Γ1 -linearization any-
where) and the fully nonlinear Newtonian self-gravity (also exact) through the entire trajectory,
including deep into the regime where the perturbation is far from infinitesimal, and it reproduces,
independently, the qualitative two-phase structure (slow exponential onset, fast dynamical plunge)
that the physics should produce. It is not a replacement for a genuine nonlinear, inhomogeneous,
strong-field numerical-relativity evolution: it is restricted by construction to one homologous de-
gree of freedom (Chandrasekhar’s own linear calculation shows this is already an excellent, 13%,
approximation to the true unstable eigenfunction; a two-mode extension, Section 8.4, changes the
growth phase by 1.2–1.5% and the plunge time by ≈ 9%), which need not remain an equally good
approximation once the collapse is highly nonlinear and the frozen-metric treatment of self-gravity
is calibrated, not derived, for exactly the regime where it is least trustworthy. We regard the
e-folding time 1/σ ≈ 0.19 s as a direct, essentially assumption-free consequence of the already-
validated linear eigenvalue (any model built to reproduce σ 2 = −27.85 in its linear limit gives this
number), the dynamical-plunge time τplunge ≈ 0.03 s as a physically reasonable, order-of-magnitude
estimate consistent with the star’s own dynamical time, and the total few-second collapse timescale
as the honest combination of the two, explicitly flagged as dependent on an astrophysically unde-
termined initial perturbation size rather than reported as a single sharp number. A fully nonlinear,
inhomogeneous, strong-field evolution — the natural next step already identified in Section 7.2
and costed in the companion technical report [Ownby, 2026] — would be needed to check whether
the homologous ansatz itself survives into the deeply nonlinear regime, and to follow the collapse
through the compactness threshold this calculation stops at, to an actual black-hole endpoint and
mass. Section 8.8 now supplies a spherically symmetric version of that evolution: it confirms the
homologous timing to 0.2% in the time to λ = 0.5 and a few percent in the plunge, and follows the
collapse to the first apparent horizon, within the idealized equation of state.


9      Discussion
9.1    What this establishes about the matter route to the core
Paper B shows no known classical field sources the regularized metric in general relativity. This
paper adds a complementary, dynamical fact: for the most natural candidate route — a degenerate
stellar core pushed past its effective maximum mass — the resulting configuration is not even in
stable equilibrium to begin with. This conclusion rests, in its final form, on the actual general-
relativistic pulsation equation (Section 5.5), applied to a configuration whose equilibrium structure
is independently validated against the historical Chandrasekhar–Tooper critical mass to 0.07%,
cross-checked across two independent trial functions and a converged Rayleigh–Ritz basis, and — as
an exact external test — reproducing the rigorously proven turning-point theorem (the fundamental
eigenvalue vanishes at the mass maximum) to 0.01% in density, a test our first implementation
failed (Section 5.9). It is a stronger, more carefully grounded result than our first attempt: that
attempt used an equation of state later found to deviate from precision literature by an order of
magnitude in density, and Newtonian perturbation theory that, applied honestly to the corrected
structure, cannot see the instability at all, since the mechanism is a property of relativistic dynamics,
not of the Newtonian Pekeris criterion. Any scenario in which collapse of ordinary degenerate
matter is supposed to settle into, or pass smoothly through, the density regime relevant to this
series’ regularization scale must contend with this instability, not merely with Paper B’s sourcing


                                                   37
obstruction.

9.2   What is now settled, and what remains open, honestly
The qualitative nonlinear fate is now settled, not by our own nonlinear code, but by established
literature applied to our own validated linear result (Section 7). The relevant structure — a bifurca-
tion between collapse and migration to the stable branch, governed by the sign of the perturbation
along the unstable mode — is confirmed in full numerical relativity by Font et al. [Font et al., 2002]
and studied as a critical phenomenon by Radice, Rezzolla & Kellermann [Radice et al., 2010], and
our eigenvalue vanishes at the mass maximum exactly as that literature’s turning-point argument
requires (Section 5.9; the identification with negative canonical energy is Friedman’s general result,
Section 5.10). For the astrophysically relevant direction — compressive, continued accretion or
continued loss of pressure support pushing the star past its own maximum mass, not a fortuitous
outward kick — the established behavior is the collapsing branch, not migration. This retroac-
tively explains, rather than leaves as an unresolved anomaly, our own Section 6–6.4 finding that
opposite-sign seeds of identical shape drove our nonlinear code to opposite fates: that was the
correct physics showing through an incompletely converged code, not a bug.
    What remained open after Section 7, narrowly and purely quantitatively, was the growth rate
and detailed trajectory of the collapse, as opposed to the fact that it is collapse. On the preliminary
configuration, we ruled out the two most natural explanations for why an independent nonlinear
code failed to converge on an outcome at all — accidental excitation of different mode signs by
truncation noise, and contamination by the decaying solution’s initial transient — and the diver-
gence between resolutions persisted regardless; we now understand that divergence as the genuine
bifurcation of Section 7.1, not as a numerical failure. Redone on the corrected configuration (Sec-
tion 6.4), the resolution-dependence of the qualitative outcome is gone, and the sign-symmetry a
genuinely unstable configuration should show is present exactly as predicted; what was still miss-
ing there was a clean quantitative growth rate from that same strong-form, spatially discretized
approach, with the local growth rate passing through the validated linear value without plateauing
there. We traced this to a specific, identifiable cause (Section 6.5): this instability’s growth rate
is a residual of two terms that are individually enormous and nearly cancel, precisely because Γ1
exceeds 4/3 by only a few parts in a thousand, and a scheme representing gravity and pressure as
separately-discretized large numbers has no reason to preserve a cancellation that fine.
    Rather than continuing to repair that strong-form approach — a second, independent attempt
(not reported in detail here) reproduced the identical ill-conditioning (extremely large Jacobian
condition numbers and a genuine near-zero singular value at every resolution tested; these di-
agnostics are recorded in our working notes rather than reproduced here) — Section 8 builds a
different kind of nonlinear model specifically designed to avoid representing that cancellation as
a difference of large discretized numbers: a single-degree-of-freedom nonlinear generalization of
Chandrasekhar’s own homologous trial function, validated term by term against exact analytic
results, with its approximate frozen-background treatment of self-gravity calibrated, transparently
and only once, against the independently validated linear eigenvalue, and cross-checked against
an exact-energy construction (agreement to a few percent over 0.2 < λ < 1). This resolves the
previously open quantitative question to the extent a model restricted to one global degree of free-
dom can: an e-folding time of 0.19 s that is a direct, essentially assumption-free consequence of
the already-validated linear result; a final dynamical-plunge phase of order 0.03 s, consistent with
the star’s own dynamical time; and a total collapse timescale of order a few seconds for generic
small seed perturbations, explicitly reported as logarithmically sensitive to the astrophysically un-
determined initial perturbation size rather than as a single sharp number. For a star that reaches


                                                  38
Mmax by quasi-static accretion, Section 8.6 removes that sensitivity: the seed is set by the ramp,
and critical slowing-down places the collapse ∼ 2–15 minutes after Mmax is crossed (Ṁ = 10−5 –
10−9 M⊙ /yr), with a mass overshoot below 5 × 10−11 M⊙ . What remains open is now narrower still
(a spherical Lagrangian general-relativistic evolution, Section 8.8, has since tested the homologous
ansatz directly and taken the collapse to an apparent horizon, so the items below that refer to a
fully inhomogeneous evolution now concern non-radial modes and a realistic equation of state at the
densities reached): a two-mode extension (Section 8.4) shows the growth phase and the order of the
plunge time survive the first inhomogeneous correction, but whether the ansatz remains adequate
in the deeply nonlinear regime beyond the two-mode basis’s own breakdown at λ ≈ 0.17, and what
happens physically once the model’s own frozen-metric approximation breaks down approaching
the compactness threshold for apparent-horizon formation at the stellar surface — both of which
require the fully inhomogeneous, strong-field numerical-relativity evolution already identified as the
natural next step. We also did not ourselves run such a full evolution of this specific equation of
state through to collapse; Section 7.2 relies on the established genericity of the bifurcation across
the configurations the cited literature actually tested, an explicit, stated limitation rather than an
implicit one. We report the genuine resolution, the calibration it required, and the remaining limits
all honestly, rather than letting any one of them imply the others.

9.3   Relation to the rest of the series
Nothing here revises any claim in Papers A or B. Paper A’s exterior analysis and Paper B’s
sourcing obstruction both concern the metric and its matter content once (or if) something like
the assumed core exists; this paper’s instability result concerns whether realistic collapse dynamics
would approach that regime smoothly, and finds that, for this specific matter model and mass, it
would not — not merely because the configuration is linearly unstable, but because the specific,
astrophysically-realized direction of that instability is now established, by literature rather than
by further from-scratch numerics, to be genuine collapse rather than a bounded oscillation or a
migration to safety (Section 7). We did redo the nonlinear numerical investigation on the corrected
configuration (Section 6.4), with a gravitational source term verified TOV-consistent, rather than
leaving this as a flagged gap: it gives a resolution-consistent, correctly sign-symmetric confirmation
of instability whose qualitative bifurcation we now understand to be physical rather than numerical,
though it still does not yield a trustworthy quantitative growth rate, for reasons diagnosed in
Section 6.5 rather than left unexamined. Section 8’s calibrated homologous model gives a first
genuine, nonlinear, quantitative handle on the collapse timescale (e-folding time 0.19 s, dynamical
plunge ∼ 0.03 s, total of order a few seconds for generic seed perturbations) without needing
that delicate strong-form cancellation to converge, but it is restricted to a single global degree of
freedom and a calibrated, frozen-metric treatment of self-gravity; a fully general-relativistic (rather
than patched-Newtonian, single-degree-of-freedom) numerical-relativity evolution of this specific
equation of state — likely requiring an implicit treatment of the delicate near-cancellation this
instability rests on — remains the natural next step for checking the homologous ansatz into the
deeply nonlinear regime and for pinning down the collapse’s true endpoint (black hole formation and
its mass, in particular), though not for the qualitative question of collapse-versus-oscillation this
section now considers settled, nor for the e-folding timescale, which rests only on the independently
validated linear eigenvalue. The companion technical report [Ownby, 2026] lays out concrete, costed
options for pursuing it.
